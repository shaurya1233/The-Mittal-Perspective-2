POLITICS & AI

Department briefing · Independent editorial

Artificial intelligence is often described as a contest between companies, models and countries. Beneath that contest lies an institutional question: who sets the rules, who builds shared infrastructure, who protects citizens and who is accountable when automated systems cause harm?

01 · The state's proper role

Set the conditions. Do not attempt to script every outcome.

Political leadership shapes the environment in which AI is researched, financed, deployed and adopted. Governments establish rights and obligations, fund education and research, procure technology, build digital infrastructure and decide how public services use automated systems. Those decisions can widen opportunity or create barriers that favour only the largest organisations.

The best public role is neither passive nor overbearing. Government should establish predictable rules, invest in public capabilities, enable fair competition, protect citizens and remain accountable for decisions made in its name. It should not direct every technical choice or use regulation to reward political allies.

01

Protect rights and public trust

Set enforceable expectations for privacy, security, transparency, safety and meaningful human review in consequential decisions.

02

Build shared foundations

Support connectivity, research institutions, compute access, interoperable public infrastructure and high-quality datasets with appropriate safeguards.

03

Keep public procurement clean

Publish clear tenders, evaluation criteria, conflict-of-interest safeguards and measurable outcomes for public technology contracts.

04

Make competition meaningful

Ensure smaller firms, universities and public-interest innovators can participate without requiring political access or disproportionate compliance budgets.

05

Prepare people for transition

Invest in teachers, vocational pathways, research careers, digital literacy and lifelong learning—not only technology announcements.

02 · The public value test

Measure outcomes, not announcements.

A new compute cluster, AI centre or public-private partnership is an input. Its value should be assessed by what it enables: useful research, accessible services, local-language tools, improved productivity, new businesses and better public outcomes. Every public programme should publish a clear purpose, a delivery timetable, an independent evaluation method and a route for citizens to raise concerns.

PUBLIC SERVICE

Human-centred deployment

Use AI to assist professionals in education, health, agriculture and administration, while preserving a human route to review and appeal important decisions.

PUBLIC ACCOUNTABILITY

Transparent systems

Disclose when automated systems materially influence services, explain their limitations and audit them for errors and unequal impact.

ECONOMIC RESILIENCE

Open participation

Support local innovation, small businesses, universities and regional-language builders rather than concentrating every opportunity in a few hubs.

DEMOCRATIC TRUST

Independent scrutiny

Allow journalists, researchers, civil society and affected citizens to examine public technology programmes without intimidation.

03 · A standard for political leadership

Technology policy must serve citizens before institutions serve themselves.

Public office carries a duty to act lawfully, disclose relevant interests, use public money responsibly and explain decisions. Technology procurement should never become a shortcut to private favour. Nor should AI be used to manufacture public consent, impersonate people or obscure responsibility.

The measure of a technology policy is not how impressive it sounds at launch. It is whether people are better served after it is implemented.The Mittal Perspective · Editorial principle

India's AI future needs ambition and restraint in equal measure: the ambition to create globally relevant technology, and the restraint to test it, govern it and correct it when necessary.

Build capability. Protect citizens. Keep power accountable.

INDIA’S YOUTH

Department briefing · Independent editorial

A country cannot build a resilient technology economy by teaching its young people only how to consume applications. Students need strong foundations, the confidence to question automated outputs, the ability to create useful solutions and access to mentors who can help turn curiosity into capability.

01 · Education before automation

AI should strengthen learning—not replace the work of learning.

AI tools can help learners explore difficult concepts, practise languages, receive feedback and discover new subjects. They can also produce confident errors, conceal weak understanding and make copied work appear original. The answer is not blind adoption or blanket rejection. It is education that combines technical knowledge with judgement.

01

Keep the foundations strong

Mathematics, science, language, reading, writing, logic and communication remain essential. Tools cannot substitute for understanding.

02

Teach AI literacy

Explain, at an age-appropriate level, how models learn, why outputs can be wrong, how data is used and how to verify claims.

03

Build practical judgement

Ask students to compare sources, identify uncertainty, disclose AI assistance and explain their own reasoning.

04

Train the teachers

Provide educators with time, professional development and classroom guidance before expecting them to supervise new tools.

02 · Opportunity across India

Talent is everywhere. Access is not.

Access to quality devices, stable internet, labs, mentors and internships is uneven. A serious national strategy should not assume that every young person studies in a major technology centre or has the same family resources.

ACCESS

Regional learning hubs

Support labs, libraries and shared learning spaces in smaller cities and rural communities.

LANGUAGE

Local-language resources

Make high-quality AI and digital education understandable beyond a small set of English-speaking communities.

EXPERIENCE

Meaningful apprenticeships

Give learners supervised experience on real problems, with clear expectations and fair evaluation.

CREATION

Public-interest projects

Encourage work in agriculture, accessibility, climate resilience, education and local enterprise.

03 · A practical development pathway

From curious student to capable contributor.

  1. Explore: build curiosity through reading, experiments, problem-solving and exposure to real-world issues.
  2. Understand: develop strong academic foundations and learn the limits of digital tools.
  3. Create: complete small projects that solve a clearly defined problem and can be tested by real users.
  4. Communicate: explain the work clearly, document decisions and accept critical feedback.
  5. Contribute: collaborate with others, protect users and improve a project over time.
A young citizen should leave school able to question technology, not merely operate it.A principle for India's next generation
04 · Shared responsibility

Government, educators and employers must work together.

Political leadership can support teacher training, public-interest research and transparent pathways into work. Universities can connect theory with practice. Businesses can offer genuine entry-level opportunities. Families can encourage healthy digital habits. Young people can bring curiosity, discipline and a willingness to learn.

Success should be measured not only by the number of people trained, but by whether they can apply their skills, find meaningful work, build useful organisations and participate safely in public life.

Do not prepare the next generation merely to use the future. Prepare them to build it responsibly.

DIGITAL GUARDIANS

Department briefing · Independent editorial

Digital services can open doors to learning, communication and creativity. They can also expose children to manipulation, harassment, privacy loss, scams and designs that encourage excessive use. Parents and schools should not be expected to compensate for unsafe platform design on their own.

01 · A principled approach

Under-17 safeguards should be strong, proportionate and evidence-led.

This publication proposes a graduated digital-safety framework for people under 17. It should protect children while preserving legitimate access to education, communication, creativity and public information. A blanket ban on every service or every teenager is not the only policy option, and may create its own problems if it pushes activity into less visible spaces.

Important legal distinction

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act and the DPDP Rules, 2025 establish a framework that includes special treatment of children's personal data and verifiable parental or guardian consent, subject to the law's definitions, exemptions and phased implementation. This is not the same as a blanket legal prohibition on all under-17s using every social-media app. Check current official rules before making compliance decisions.

02 · Social-media protections

Make safer behaviour the default—not a hidden setting.

01

Age-appropriate experiences

Use graduated protections that recognise differences between younger children and older teenagers, rather than treating every age alike.

02

Private-by-default accounts

Limit unwanted contact, public discoverability and location exposure unless a young user and guardian make an informed choice where appropriate.

03

Less manipulative design

Consider limits on autoplay, endless-scroll pressure, late-night notifications and engagement features that make it difficult to take breaks.

04

Recommendation controls

Provide understandable controls and reduce amplification of exploitative, dangerous or age-inappropriate material.

05

Meaningful reporting

Make reporting, blocking and appeals easy to find, and require timely responses to serious safety complaints.

06

Privacy-respecting age assurance

Use proportionate methods and avoid collecting identity documents or sensitive information when less intrusive approaches can work.

03 · Payment-app safeguards

Teach financial independence without exposing children to avoidable risk.

Payment technology can help young people understand budgeting and transactions. It can also make impulsive spending, impersonation scams and accidental disclosure easier. Policy should distinguish supervised, age-appropriate payment functions from unrestricted credit, borrowing or complex financial products.

Possible safeguards include guardian visibility where appropriate, age-suitable limits, clear transaction alerts, strong authentication, simple fraud reporting and plain-language financial education. Credit and high-risk financial products should not be marketed to children as shortcuts to status or wealth.

Permissions depend on current banking, payment-provider and regulatory rules. A bank may offer a minor's account under applicable conditions; that does not automatically make every payment app or feature available to every minor. Families should check the rules of the bank and service before enabling access.

04 · How to make safeguards work

Bring young people into the conversation.

Rules are more likely to work when they are developed with child-development specialists, educators, parents, technology companies and young people themselves. Policymakers should assess effectiveness, publish evidence, protect privacy and review unintended consequences. Schools should teach digital citizenship without shaming students. Platforms should be responsible for the systems they design.

A safe digital environment should not require a child to become an expert in avoiding harm before they can participate.The Mittal Perspective · Policy proposal
Protect childhood. Preserve opportunity. Design technology with care.

ENTERPRISE

Department briefing · Independent editorial

Popularity is an outcome, not a business model. A durable enterprise starts with a problem that matters, a customer who recognises it and a solution worth paying for. Attention can introduce a business; only consistent value and trustworthy delivery can sustain it.

01 · The foundation

Build around a real customer—not an imagined market.

01

Discover the problem

Speak with the people affected. Observe the work they already do and learn what they have tried before.

02

Define a clear offer

Explain who the service helps, what it includes, what it costs and what it does not promise.

03

Deliver consistently

Document the process, test before delivery, meet agreed deadlines and make support easy to reach.

04

Know the economics

Track cost of delivery, time, refunds, taxes, cash flow and the margin left after each sale.

05

Earn trust honestly

Never invent customers, testimonials, awards, revenue, partnerships or capabilities. Evidence is stronger than theatre.

06

Improve before expanding

Make the core offer repeatable before adding new services, hiring or taking on large fixed costs.

02 · Reputation without vanity

Become known for the quality of your thinking and delivery.

Publish useful explanations. Show the process behind a genuine result. Share lessons from real work, including what did not go to plan. Give credit to collaborators, protect client information and correct public mistakes. Ask customers what improved for them and use feedback to make the offer better.

Followers, views and polished visuals can help distribution, but they do not prove quality. A smaller group of satisfied customers who return and refer others is often more valuable than a large audience that never trusts the offer.

Do not chase attention so hard that you neglect the people who already trusted you.Enterprise principle
03 · Commercial discipline

Understand the numbers before you chase scale.

REVENUE QUALITY

Repeatable demand

Learn whether customers return, renew or refer—not just whether a first sale can be made.

DELIVERY QUALITY

Operational reliability

Track delivery time, defects, revisions, support requests and customer satisfaction.

FINANCIAL HEALTH

Cash discipline

Separate business and personal records, plan for taxes and keep enough liquidity for obligations.

STRATEGIC FOCUS

One excellent offer

Do not add complexity faster than the organisation can maintain quality.

04 · A disciplined first 90 days

Move from idea to evidence.

  1. Days 01–30Research and learnChoose a specific audience, speak with potential customers, compare existing solutions and write a one-page problem statement.
  2. Days 31–60Build and testCreate a focused first version. Test it with a small group, ask for direct feedback and record what fails.
  3. Days 61–90Improve and measureRefine the offer, publish clear terms, set a realistic price, track costs and decide whether customers genuinely want the next version.
05 · What responsible growth looks like

Scale only what you can stand behind.

Hiring, new markets, partnerships and large campaigns should follow evidence of repeatable demand. Before expanding, ask whether delivery quality will remain stable, whether the organisation can meet its obligations and whether the additional complexity serves the customer.

Long-term business value is built through useful products, clear contracts, reliable support, fair treatment and disciplined management. A founder's reputation is not separate from the company; it is reflected in how the company behaves when something goes wrong.

Build something useful. Deliver what you promise. Let reputation compound.

CAPITAL & WEALTH

Department briefing · Independent editorial

Capital is a resource, not a status symbol. It should be allocated with a clear purpose, an understanding of risk and respect for the obligations that come first. For a young entrepreneur, the most valuable early investments are often capability, customer insight and the reliability of the work itself.

01 · Three distinct forms of investment

Do not confuse personal finance with business spending.

01 / HUMAN CAPITAL

Invest in capability

Build communication, mathematics, financial literacy, technical skills, sales judgement and the ability to learn independently.

02 / BUSINESS CAPITAL

Remove proven bottlenecks

Spend on security, reliable delivery, quality assurance, customer support or tools only when they solve a demonstrated problem.

03 / PERSONAL CAPITAL

Make regulated decisions carefully

Understand product risks, fees, liquidity, taxes and time horizon. Seek appropriately qualified advice when needed.

02 · Principles of prudent stewardship

Protect the downside before pursuing the upside.

  1. Understand before committing: never put money into a product you cannot explain in plain language.
  2. Respect liquidity: do not tie up money that may be needed for near-term obligations or emergencies.
  3. Recognise uncertainty: returns are not guaranteed, and past performance does not establish future results.
  4. Avoid leverage and speculation: borrowing to chase uncertain gains can magnify losses and create lasting pressure.
  5. Verify every intermediary: use official regulator resources and check the registration status of professionals where applicable.
  6. Keep records: understand fees, taxes, ownership, account access and the terms of every transaction.
03 · A young person's perspective

Build financial judgement before chasing financial returns.

For a minor, financial decisions should involve a parent or legal guardian and follow the rules that apply to the relevant account and product. Do not use money needed for education or essential expenses, do not borrow to speculate and do not follow anonymous tips, urgency tactics or guaranteed-return claims.

Educational disclosure

This page is general financial education, not personal investment advice, a recommendation to buy or sell any security, or a promise of returns. Product suitability depends on individual circumstances and applicable rules. Consult official investor-education resources and a properly qualified professional when appropriate.

04 · A business capital checklist

Before spending, ask five questions.

  1. What exact business problem will this expense solve?
  2. What evidence shows the problem is affecting customers or delivery?
  3. Is there a lower-cost way to test the proposed solution?
  4. How will the result be measured and when will it be reviewed?
  5. Can the business afford the expense without compromising its obligations?
The first responsibility of capital is to be understood. The second is to be used with discipline.The Mittal Perspective · Capital principle
Create value patiently. Manage risk honestly. Let discipline guide capital.

LEADERSHIP

Department briefing · Independent editorial

Leadership is not the art of appearing important. It is the responsibility to make decisions that affect people, resources and the future of an organisation. The higher the authority, the stronger the obligation to explain decisions, listen to evidence and accept scrutiny.

01 · The leadership contract

Authority is a responsibility before it is a privilege.

01

Set a clear direction

Explain the purpose, priorities and trade-offs so people understand what the organisation is trying to achieve.

02

Choose people carefully

Build capable teams, define responsibilities and create an environment where expertise can challenge assumptions.

03

Allocate resources honestly

Match budgets and time to declared priorities. Disclose conflicts and avoid decisions driven by personal favour.

04

Measure what matters

Use a small set of meaningful measures: quality, customer outcomes, financial health, safety and delivery reliability.

05

Correct mistakes openly

Listen to criticism, preserve evidence, explain changes and fix the underlying process—not only the public message.

02 · What a chairman or CEO should protect

Governance makes ambition durable.

In a well-governed organisation, the board provides oversight and strategic guidance; executive leadership manages operations and delivery; internal controls and independent review help identify risk. The precise division depends on the organisation's legal structure and applicable law, but the central principle is consistent: authority should have defined boundaries and accountability.

STRATEGY

Long-term direction

Set priorities, understand market changes and decide what the organisation will not pursue.

RISK

Controls and resilience

Protect data, finances, people, reputation and business continuity.

CULTURE

Standards of conduct

Make honesty, fair treatment and responsible escalation normal parts of daily work.

STAKEHOLDERS

Trust beyond the boardroom

Respect customers, employees, partners, communities and the wider public.

03 · Decision quality

Use a disciplined decision framework.

  1. Define: state the decision and the problem it is meant to solve.
  2. Verify: separate facts, assumptions, forecasts and unknowns.
  3. Compare: consider alternatives, costs, risks and who may be affected.
  4. Decide: name the responsible decision-maker and document the reasoning.
  5. Review: measure results, hear dissent and change course when evidence requires it.
04 · Executive presence without theatre

Professionalism is visible in preparation and conduct.

Strong executive communication is clear, measured and specific. It avoids inflated claims, false urgency and unnecessary jargon. A leader should know the numbers, understand the subject, credit the team, disclose uncertainty and make commitments that can be honoured.

The strongest reputation is built when an organisation behaves responsibly even when no one is watching.The Mittal Perspective · Leadership standard
Lead with judgement. Govern with integrity. Be accountable for outcomes.

ABOUT THE JOURNAL

Department briefing · Independent editorial

The Mittal Perspective is an independent editorial publication built around a simple belief: serious ideas deserve clear writing, responsible evidence and a long-term point of view. It explores how public policy, technology, enterprise and leadership influence the choices available to people and communities.

01 · Editorial mission

Make complex subjects clearer without making them simplistic.

The journal aims to connect policy with its human consequences, business strategy with ethical responsibility and technological possibility with practical governance. It favours careful reasoning over spectacle, useful analysis over empty slogans and long-term value over short-lived attention.

01

Independent perspective

Editorial views are presented as analysis and opinion, not as official statements or endorsements.

02

Evidence-aware writing

Factual claims should be traceable to credible sources. Unverified material should be identified as such.

03

Responsible ambition

Explore ambitious ideas while acknowledging constraints, trade-offs and the responsibilities that accompany influence.

04

Useful knowledge

Translate broad ideas into questions, principles and practical frameworks readers can evaluate.

02 · How claims are treated

Three different kinds of statements.

DOCUMENTED INFORMATION

Facts and policy

Claims about published rules, government programmes or official guidance should be linked to the relevant primary source and dated where useful.

EDITORIAL ANALYSIS

Interpretation

Analysis explains what a development may mean. It remains an argument, not a proven outcome or official position.

POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

Proposals

Recommendations describe what the author believes should be considered. They must not be mistaken for law currently in force.

03 · Corrections and independence

Accuracy is a continuing responsibility.

Public policy, regulatory rules and programme figures can change. Readers should verify current legal requirements and official data at the source before relying on them. If an article contains an error, the correction should identify what changed and why. The publication should not imply endorsement by any government, public official, company or institution without explicit, verifiable authorisation.

Financial and legal disclaimer

The journal does not provide legal, tax, investment or personalised financial advice. Educational material is general in nature. Readers should consult official authorities and qualified professionals for decisions affecting their circumstances.

Trust is not a design feature. It is a standard of evidence and conduct.
